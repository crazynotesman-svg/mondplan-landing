Moon & Health - Evidence Review

Moon and Sleep

Does a full moon really keep you up? We reviewed the scientific evidence - the famous 2013 laboratory study, the 2014 replication that found nothing, and the 2021 field research that proposed a simple light-based explanation.

3 key studies, cited with DOIs Balanced: what we know & what we don't

The three studies that shaped the story

2013 - Cajochen et al., Current Biology

A Swiss team re-analyzed 64 nights of strictly controlled sleep-lab data from 33 volunteers (ages 20-74) who slept in windowless rooms with no time cues. Near full moon they found: deep-sleep (NREM delta) activity down 30%, time to fall asleep +5 minutes, total sleep -20 minutes, lower evening melatonin, and lower self-rated sleep quality. It was the first peer-reviewed evidence of a lunar rhythm in human sleep under controlled conditions.

Cajochen, C. et al. Current Biology 23(15):1485-8, 2013. DOI: 10.1016/j.cub.2013.06.029

2014 - The replication that didn't reproduce

A larger analysis drawing on data from over 1,000 participants looked for the same pattern and did not find it. This is the normal, healthy way science handles a striking small-sample result - try again with more people. For several years the reasonable conclusion was that there was probably no meaningful lunar sleep effect.

Notable as a cautionary tale about small samples and multiple testing.

2021 - Casiraghi et al., Science Advances

Field research tracked sleep in three Indigenous Argentine communities with differing access to electric light, plus university students in a US city. Across all groups, in the 3-5 nights before full moon, people went to bed later and slept less - stronger where there was no electricity, but present even in the city. Crucially, the effect peaked before the full moon, pointing to a light-based mechanism.

Casiraghi, L. et al. Science Advances 7(5), 2021. DOI: 10.1126/sciadv.abe0465

Why might it happen?

The most likely explanation is light, not lunar gravity.

Light exposure (leading hypothesis)

Moonrise shifts about 50 minutes later each day. In the nights before a full moon, the moon is already bright and rises in the early evening - adding several hours of natural light exactly when people wind down. Evening light suppresses melatonin and delays sleep onset, just like any light source.

An internal 'circalunar' clock

The 2013 lab study found effects even with no visual access to the moon, hinting humans might retain a circalunar rhythm from evolutionary history - analogous to lunar rhythms documented in marine species. This remains speculative and hard to test.

Gravity / tidal forces (largely ruled out)

The moon's gravitational pull on a human body is negligible - far weaker than, say, sitting up in bed. Tidal-force explanations are not supported by the evidence and are not taken seriously in the current literature.

The honest bottom line

Yes, but small. When studies do find a lunar sleep effect, it is modest - roughly 20 minutes of sleep and a small dip in deep sleep around full moon. Many people notice no change at all. A 2019 review of nearly 18,000 patient records found no link between lunar phase and psychiatric admissions.

Light is the plausible mechanism. The 2021 field study's 'pre-full-moon' timing fits a simple explanation: bright moonlit evenings delay bedtime in people sensitive to evening light - the same reason we avoid phone screens before bed.

What this means for you: if you feel you sleep worse around a full moon, the practical fix is the same as for any bright evening - blackout curtains or an eye mask, dim screens before bed, and a consistent bedtime. Frequently asked questions

Does the full moon really affect sleep?

Several studies report modest effects: around full moon, people may take slightly longer to fall asleep, sleep a little less, and get less deep sleep. The 2013 laboratory study (Cajochen et al.) found about 20 minutes less total sleep and 30% less deep-sleep activity near full moon. However, effects are small and not everyone is affected - lifestyle factors usually matter far more.

Why would the moon affect sleep if you can't see it?

The 2013 study was done in a windowless laboratory, yet still found effects, suggesting a possible endogenous rhythm. But the leading hypothesis from 2021 field research is simpler: in the nights before a full moon, the moon rises in the early evening and provides several hours of natural light, which may delay bedtime in people sensitive to evening light.

Is the lunar sleep effect large?

No. Even in the studies that found an effect, it was modest - roughly 20 minutes of sleep and a measurable but small reduction in deep sleep. A 2014 replication with over 1,000 participants did not reproduce the original finding, so the evidence is mixed and the effect is not considered clinically significant for most people.

Does the full moon affect mood or mental health?

A large 2019 review of nearly 18,000 patient records found no association between lunar phase and psychiatric admissions or hospital stays. Sleep is linked to mood, so any small sleep effect could in theory ripple into how people feel, but the direct evidence for a 'lunar mood effect' is weak.

How can I sleep better around the full moon?

Treat it like any bright-evening situation: use blackout curtains or an eye mask, keep screens dim in the hour before bed, keep a consistent bedtime, and avoid caffeine late in the day.
